# Own the substrate

**Why a personal memory system stays relevant across AI generations — because
it presupposes their obsolescence.**

Nicolas Noguera · Souvenance · draft v0.2 · August 2026

---

## Abstract

Personal AI memory is being built the wrong way round. Systems are coupled to
the model generation that exists when they are written, so each leap in
capability threatens to obsolete them. We argue for the inverse architecture:
an append-only event log owned by the subject, from which every intelligent
layer is a recomputable projection. Under this design, progress in AI is not a
threat but an input — a better engine re-reads the same life and produces a
better memory of it.

The architecture rests on a distinction that most memory products collapse:
between the immutable **substrate** and the lossy **projections** computed from
it. We show that this distinction is not a rhetorical convenience but the
design that industrial-scale systems converged on independently — Alibaba's
production ranking system retains up to 54,000 lifelong user behaviours and
retrieves them *conditionally*, outperforming the fixed-size consolidated
memory it replaced by 7.1% CTR in live A/B tests [6,7]. Deleting the substrate
and keeping only the distillation is the architecture that measurably failed.

We then state the strongest objections we have found — retrieval degradation at
scale, incompatibility with the neuroscience of forgetting, the claim that the
AI rather than the log authors the value, and the historical failure of
lifelogging — and answer three while conceding the fourth. The concession is
the important one: the dominant reason people abandon personal tracking is the
cost of collecting and integrating data, cited by 45.6%, 42.9% and 57.1% of
abandoners across three domains [9]. That is a behavioural problem, and no
architecture solves it.

**This is a position paper. It argues; it does not measure.** Every empirical
claim is sourced to a primary document that was checked. Where no evidence
exists, we say so rather than assert.

## 1. The asymmetry

Two things are moving at very different speeds.

Model capability is improving fast and commoditising faster. What required a
frontier model in 2024 runs on a laptop in 2026. The direction is not in
dispute; only the slope is.

Lived experience does not work that way. A conversation that was not recorded
is not recoverable at any price. The reasoning behind a decision, once
forgotten, cannot be regenerated by a larger model. There is no scaling law for
a life that was not written down.

This produces an asymmetry that most product architecture ignores: **as the
processing gets cheap, the scarcity migrates to the corpus.**

Which raises the paradox this paper exists to resolve. If tomorrow's AI will be
far better than today's, why build a memory system now? Why not wait?

The answer is that the two halves of the system have opposite time constants.
The intelligent half should be waited for, and will arrive on its own. The
corpus half accrues only in real time, and every year you wait is a year that
never gets recorded. Building now is not a bet that today's models are good
enough. It is a bet that **they aren't, and that this is precisely why the
architecture must not depend on them.**

## 2. What is actually being built

### 2.1 Memory in consumer AI products

The state of practice is no longer speculative. Dash et al. (2026) analysed
2,050 memory entries from 80 real ChatGPT users and found that **96% were
created unilaterally by the system**, with only 4% initiated by the user
through an explicit instruction. 52% contained psychological inferences —
beliefs, desires, fears, intentions, classified through a theory-of-mind
framework — and 28% contained GDPR-defined personal data. 84% were factually
grounded in the conversation that produced them [1].

Two things follow, pointing in opposite directions.

These systems work. An 84% grounding rate is not a system hallucinating a user;
it is one reading them accurately. The inference layer does real work.

But the artefact belongs to the vendor's architecture, not to the subject. It
is a distillation, and the conversation that produced it is not held as a
substrate the user can re-derive from with a different engine.

Vendor architectures differ in legibility. Anthropic's memory tool operates on
plain files, and its "Dreams" pipeline — announced 6 May 2026 as a research
preview on the Managed Agents API — runs asynchronous consolidation that merges
duplicates, replaces contradicted entries with current values, and surfaces
recurring patterns [2]. That is a markedly better design than a hidden vector
profile. It remains a distillation in the vendor's format, and it matters here
for a reason we return to in §5.1: **the labs have independently concluded that
an unconsolidated memory store degrades.** They are right. That is an argument
about the projection layer, not about the substrate.

### 2.2 Memory layers for developers

A category of infrastructure now sits between application and model: Mem0,
Zep/Graphiti, Letta/MemGPT. Architecturally these externalise memory from the
model, which is the correct move.

They are evaluated primarily on LoCoMo (Maharana et al., ACL 2024): up to 35
sessions, ~300 turns, 1,540 questions across single-hop, multi-hop, temporal and
open-domain categories [3]. Temporal reasoning is consistently the weakest
category — a finding we build on in §3.4.

Two caveats, stated because the field does not state them often enough. Most
published scores are vendor self-evaluations with rare independent
reproduction, so we cite LoCoMo as a benchmark design rather than reproducing
any leaderboard. And there is a distribution-mismatch critique with teeth:
LoCoMo simulates friends chatting, and a high score there does not transfer
automatically to other domains.

The limit that matters most, though, is one the benchmark cannot address.
**LoCoMo caps at 35 sessions.** Nothing in this literature evaluates a decade.
For evidence at that scale we have to leave the memory-systems literature
entirely — which is what §3.5 does.

### 2.3 The lifelogging lineage, and its constructive critic

The idea is old: Bush's Memex (1945), Bell's MyLifeBits, and the 2024–2026
wearable generation. The most useful source in this lineage is not a system but
a critique.

Sellen and Whittaker, *Beyond Total Capture: A Constructive Critique of
Lifelogging* (CACM, May 2010), argued that indiscriminate capture is
psychologically misconceived: human memory is not a defective store needing an
exhaustive substitute but a selective, reconstructive system, and archives are
not memories — they are **cues** that must be selected and indexed to trigger
biological recall. They proposed five functions any such system should serve:
recollecting, reminiscing, retrieving, reflecting, and remembering intentions
[4].

The empirical record supports them on retrieval. The NTCIR-Lifelog evaluations
(Gurrin et al., Dublin City University) built standardised lifelog corpora —
tens of thousands of passively captured images per user with location,
biometric and activity metadata — and found that off-the-shelf visual concept
extraction failed to close the semantic gap: precision collapsed as soon as
queries involved human intention, temporal sequencing or task attribution [5].
Raw capture without semantic modelling does not produce a searchable memory.

We take this as **support for the architecture, not against it.** Sellen and
Whittaker's target is a system whose interface is the raw archive. The response
is not to capture less; it is to keep the archive as substrate and make the
*interface* selective — which is the projection layer. Where their critique
genuinely lands is on capture cost, and we concede that in §5.4.

## 3. The substrate thesis

### 3.1 Events are records; memories are projections

The architecture has one invariant: **the append-only event log is the only
thing that is authoritative.** Everything else — extracted memories, embeddings,
the knowledge graph, summaries, the persona — is a projection computed from it,
and every projection is disposable by construction.

This is event sourcing applied to a life, and its consequence is the whole
thesis: when a better model arrives, you do not migrate the memory. You
**re-project** it. The same events, re-read by a better engine, yield a better
memory. The upgrade path for the intelligent layer is drop-and-recompute, and
that is a feature.

Contrast a system whose authoritative artefact is the distillation. A better
model there can improve future extractions but not past ones, because the
material they came from is gone. Such a system's memory quality is permanently
pinned to the model generation current when each memory formed. It accumulates
strata of unequal quality that can never be levelled.

Two corollaries held as design rules:

**Originals forever.** The raw signal — audio, images, source files — is
retained after processing. If 2026's transcription is mediocre, 2036's is not
stuck with it.

**Capture must never depend on the intelligent layer.** If the model is down or
the vendor vanished, capture continues and processing queues. The failure mode
of the AI layer is *pause*, never *loss*.

### 3.2 What survives a generation, and what does not

**Survives:** the event log; the originals; provenance; validity intervals on
facts; the subject's governance decisions — exclusions, corrections,
testamentary clauses.

**Does not survive, and should not:** extraction quality, embedding space,
prompt design, the decomposition into "memories", summarisation style.

The second list is the entire visible surface of most memory products. That is
the point. **The disposable parts are the ones users see; the durable parts are
the ones they are not offered.**

### 3.3 The adapter argument

The AI layer is a plug. Swapping the consolidation model should be a
configuration change; swapping the embedding backend should require re-indexing
but not re-capturing.

This has a falsifiable implication: if replacing the model requires touching
anything beyond configuration and recomputation, the coupling was real and the
architecture failed its own test.

### 3.4 Lived time as a durable primitive

Models have no clock. Time enters a transformer as positional encoding within a
context window — an ordering, not a duration. This is why temporal reasoning is
LoCoMo's weakest category [3], and the failure is structural.

The architectural response is to compute time in code: bi-temporal modelling,
distinguishing when something was true in the world (valid time) from when the
system learned it (transaction time). Facts carry validity intervals; a
contradicted fact has its interval closed rather than being deleted.

This matters beyond correctness. **Arithmetic computed in code is
model-independent by construction** — among the few properties that transfer
unchanged across every future engine. It also matters for §5.2, because closing
an interval preserves what deletion destroys: that the subject once believed
otherwise.

### 3.5 The industrial evidence: retain everything, retrieve conditionally

The strongest empirical support for this architecture comes from a field that
was not trying to build personal memory at all.

Industrial recommendation faced the same problem at extreme scale: how to use a
user's entire behavioural history when attention is quadratic in sequence
length. Alibaba's first answer, MIMN, compressed lifelong behaviour
incrementally into a **fixed-size memory matrix** and scaled to ~1,000
behaviours. Past that, the memory saturated and accumulated noise that actively
degraded click-through prediction [6]. A fixed consolidated store, continuously
updated and bounded in size, is precisely the architecture of a distilled
memory product — and it hit a ceiling.

Their replacement, SIM, abandoned compression and kept the raw sequence,
**scaling to a maximum length of 54,000 behaviours — 54× the prior state of the
art**. It retrieves in two cascaded stages: a General Search Unit filters the
full raw history against the current candidate, producing a short relevant
sub-sequence; an Exact Search Unit applies multi-head attention only to that
sub-sequence. Deployed in Alibaba's display advertising since 2019, SIM
delivered **+7.1% CTR and +4.4% RPM** in live A/B tests [7].

The result generalises past advertising because the mechanism is architectural,
not domain-specific. Long histories degrade prediction **when compressed into a
fixed global representation**; the same histories improve it **when retained
whole and queried conditionally against present context**. Retrieval must be
conditional; the substrate must be complete. That is the thesis, measured, in
production, at a scale no memory benchmark approaches.

The information-retrieval literature reaches the same conclusion by a different
route. Long-term browsing history improves search personalisation — Matthijs
and Radlinski (2011) reported roughly 9% relative MRR improvement from
long-term profiles [8] — but the gain is concentrated in ambiguous, exploratory
queries and is absent or harmful for navigational ones. History is valuable
*for disambiguation*, and only when queried dynamically.

## 4. What the labs will not do — and what the law permits

The argument here is about incentives, not capability.

Accumulated context is the retention mechanism. A user whose assistant knows
them well faces a switching cost measured in years, and that cost is the
product's most durable moat — more durable than model quality, which
competitors match within months. Portability dissolves precisely the asset that
model commoditisation makes most valuable.

The Dash finding sharpens this. When 96% of memory content is system-generated
rather than user-stated [1], the artefact is not a record of what the user chose
to preserve; it is the vendor's model *of* the user.

**And European law explicitly permits keeping it.** GDPR Article 20 grants
portability only over data "provided by" the subject. The Article 29 Working
Party guidance (WP242, endorsed by the EDPB) reads this to include *observed*
data — activity logs, location, history — but to **exclude inferred and derived
data**: profiles, scores, and algorithmic conclusions about the person [10].
Such data remains accessible under the Article 15 right of access, but carries
no obligation of machine-readable, interoperable transfer.

The Data Act (Regulation (EU) 2023/2854) codifies the same exclusion for
connected products. Recital 15 states that information inferred or derived from
raw data, "which is the outcome of additional investments into assigning values
or insights from the data, in particular by means of proprietary, complex
algorithms", falls outside the sharing obligation unless otherwise agreed [11].

This changes the character of our claim. It is not that portable memory is
technically hard, nor merely that labs lack enthusiasm. **The inference layer —
the part where the value now lives — is deliberately sanctuarised by the
regulatory framework**, on intellectual-property grounds. A substantial legal
literature contests this as self-defeating for market contestability, arguing
that a user who cannot port their algorithmic reputation or profile restarts
from zero regardless of their right to raw logs. That debate is unresolved.
What is settled is the current state: you have a right to your data, and no
right to the model of you built from it.

The argument is falsifiable — see §6.

## 5. Objections

### 5.1 The retrieval-degradation objection

**The objection.** An append-only log does not compound; it becomes a
computational liability. As a corpus grows without deletion the semantic space
crowds, and a query retrieves entries that are cosine-similar but temporally
irrelevant or superseded. The labs converged on consolidation with deletion [2]
because unbounded accumulation does not work. The 2036 engine suffocates on
2026's noise.

**The answer.** This is correct about memory stores and does not apply here,
because it collapses two layers this architecture separates.

The event log is append-only. **The memory layer is not.** Consolidation scores
importance, forgets actively, and compresses old episodic detail into gists.
The projections do what Dreams does — deduplicate, close contradicted facts,
decay the unimportant — while the substrate stays intact. Retrieval runs over
projections, never over the raw log.

This is no longer only an argument. §3.5 is the objection tested at scale and
resolved against it: MIMN's fixed consolidated memory saturated at ~1,000
items, while SIM's conditional retrieval over 54,000 raw behaviours improved
production metrics [6,7]. The system that discarded its substrate is the one
that hit the ceiling.

What the separation additionally buys is that consolidation becomes
**reversible**. A store that deletes has made an irreversible judgement using
the extraction quality available at the time. What 2026 scored as unimportant,
2036 may score as pivotal — and only a system that kept the substrate can act
on that.

**What survives.** SIM's search unit is cheap because it filters by category
and embedding dot-product against a *specific candidate item*. Personal-memory
queries are open-ended and often have no candidate to filter against. The
mechanism transfers; the cost profile may not. Whether re-projection over a
decade-scale log stays tractable is unmeasured, by us and by anyone.

### 5.2 The neurobiological objection

**The objection.** The design rests on a hard-drive metaphor that biological
memory refutes. Retrieval destabilises the trace, leading to reconsolidation.
Forgetting is a feature: Richards and Frankland (2017) argue that transience
enhances flexibility by reducing the influence of outdated information on
memory-guided decision-making, and prevents overfitting to specific past events,
promoting generalisation in noisy environments [12]. Preserving
infinite-resolution detail contradicts the best account of why memory works.

**The answer.** The most rhetorically powerful objection, and we think the
weakest, because it draws the wrong boundary around the system.

The brain forgets *because it has no external store*. Transience is an
adaptation to a substrate that is expensive, lossy and non-augmentable. A person
with a diary is not a counterexample to Richards and Frankland; they are a
cognitive system with a forgetting layer *and* a non-forgetting archive, and
nobody argues the diary should fade to promote generalisation. The architecture
is that arrangement with the archive made queryable. The forgetting layer exists
— it is the consolidation of §5.1 — and it is where the neuroscience applies.

The reversal is sharper on mechanism. Their argument is that transience prevents
overfitting to *outdated* information. But deletion is a crude encoding of
outdatedness: it destroys the difference between "this was never true" and "this
was true until March 2019". Bi-temporal modelling encodes exactly that
distinction (§3.4) — the contradicted edge is closed, not removed. The system
knows the current value *and* that a previous one was held, which is strictly
more information than forgetting provides, with the same protection against
acting on stale facts.

Reconsolidation likewise argues *for* the substrate. If every recall rewrites
the trace, a system whose only artefact is the recalled version accumulates
drift with no ground truth to correct against.

**What survives, and it is not technical.** There is a documented cost to
unfiltered confrontation with one's own past. Epstein et al. found that 16.2% of
people who abandoned physical-activity tracking cited **guilt** — an archive
that objectively reflects daily shortfalls turns reflection into a punitive
mechanism [9]. A related finding cuts deeper: the *photo-taking impairment
effect* shows that externalising an experience to a device degrades biological
memory of it, shifting encoding from the experience toward where-to-find-it.
Capture is not neutral with respect to the memory it records. This is a design
responsibility — governance, exclusion, opacity by default — and we have no
evidence about how it plays out over years.

### 5.3 The inference-ownership objection

**The objection.** The AI is not a replaceable adapter reading a log; it is the
*author* of the log's utility. 96% of memory content is unilaterally inferred
and 52% consists of psychological constructs generated by the model's theory of
mind [1]. If the value rests on a particular 2026 model's inferences, removing
it and feeding raw text to a 2036 engine guarantees nothing. The separation is
conceptually illusory.

**The answer.** The strongest objection, and the reply is that it is evidence
*for* the thesis, misread.

The Dash finding describes a system where the inference **is** the artefact: the
extracted memory is what is kept, and its source material is not held as a
re-derivable substrate. That is the failure mode this paper argues against. If
value lies in the inference, and inference improves with each generation, then
whoever holds the raw substrate captures every future improvement, and whoever
holds only 2026's inferences is frozen there permanently. The objection
establishes that inference creates the value; it does not establish that the
inference is what you should store.

Nor does the architecture claim projections are model-neutral. It claims the
opposite: they are model-dependent, therefore disposable, therefore the
authoritative artefact must be something else. "The AI authors the utility" and
"the log is the durable asset" are the premise and the conclusion of the same
argument.

**What survives, and it is real.** Capture itself is partly model-mediated:
speech becomes text through a model. If 2026's transcription is poor, the 2036
engine re-reads flawed text. The originals-forever rule (§3.1) answers this only
for modalities retained losslessly, and retention discipline erodes under
storage pressure. The substrate's purity is a *practice*, not a guarantee.

### 5.4 The capture-friction objection — conceded

**The objection.** Every previous attempt at this died, and not for
architectural reasons. The cost is in capture, and people stop.

**We concede this, and the evidence is worse than the objection states.**

Epstein et al. surveyed past and present trackers across three domains — 105
for physical activity, 99 for finances, 83 for location — and interviewed 22.
Their finding on why people stop is unambiguous and consistent: the **cost of
collecting and integrating data** was the dominant reason in all three domains,
cited by **45.6% (activity), 42.9% (location) and 57.1% (finances)** of those
who abandoned [9]. For location tracking, the cost of holding or sharing the
data was cited nearly as often (45.2%) — a privacy burden specific to
continuous capture.

This is the risk, stated in numbers, and it is behavioural rather than
technical. No architectural elegance compensates for a system that asks for
daily discipline. Every design decision in a system like this should be
evaluated first against capture friction, and we do not claim to have solved it
— only that it is the thing to optimise.

Two honest nuances. Epstein et al. also identify **"happy abandonment"**: 11.8%
of activity trackers and 14.3% of finance trackers stopped because they had
learned enough, and 7.1% of former finance trackers continued applying what they
learned after stopping. Their conclusion is that abandonment "is not always
indicative of failure nor the successful end of a process". A memory system
whose value is retrospective rather than behaviour-changing may have a different
abandonment profile than a fitness tracker — but that is a hypothesis, not a
finding.

### 5.5 Two further threats

**Context stops being scarce.** Hundred-million-token windows make consolidation
unnecessary: feed the raw log and let the model sort it. We concede fully, and
note that it *strengthens* the architecture — the intelligent layer dies, the
substrate survives and turns out to be the only thing that mattered.

**The corpus proves less valuable than assumed.** This was our largest gap and
is now partially addressed — see §5.6 — but the evidence is indirect and the
honest position is unchanged: our case rests on an argument from asymmetry (§1),
not on data about personal memory specifically, and it is unfalsifiable on a
short horizon.

### 5.6 Does old personal data stay useful? What the adjacent literature shows

There is no empirical literature on the compounding value of a *personal AI
corpus*; the field does not exist. There is a large one on the temporal dynamics
of user models, and it does not give a single answer. It gives a distinction.

**For transient behavioural states, recency dominates and old data can be
actively harmful.** This is the concept-drift literature: when the conditional
distribution shifts (*real drift*), a model trained on stale data optimises for
a distribution that no longer exists. Adaptive windowing methods (ADWIN, DDM)
exist precisely to detect this and discard history. In fast-moving consumption
domains, unweighted old data is not inert — it is toxic.

**For structural regularities, old data retains value.** Koren's timeSVD++ work
on the Netflix data found that discarding or exponentially decaying old ratings
was *sub-optimal*: separating transient noise from long-term drift beat throwing
history away. The reason is that different users drift at different rates, and
persistent structure survives underneath transient variation.

**For dispositional traits, stability is high and measured.** Roberts and
DelVecchio's meta-analysis of 152 longitudinal studies (3,217 test-retest
correlations) found rank-order consistency rising from .31 in childhood to .54
in college, .64 at age 30, and plateauing around **.74 between ages 50 and 70**
[13]. We state one caveat the secondary literature routinely drops: these
coefficients are reported **with the retest interval held constant at 6.7
years**. They license a claim about stable individual differences over years;
they do **not** license the claim that forty-year-old data predicts present
behaviour with the same fidelity, and we do not make it.

The synthesis relevant to this paper: what decays is the *state*; what persists
is the *trait* and the structural regularity. A five-year-old purchase is
worthless for predicting tomorrow's purchase and informative about how the
person decides. Applying a uniform recency decay to an entire personal profile —
the standard move — discards the durable layer to chase the volatile one.

**The limit of this evidence, stated plainly.** All of it concerns *predicting
behaviour* under a commercial objective — clicks, conversions, ranking. A
personal memory system optimises for something else: retrieval, situation,
recall of what was actually said and meant. The mechanisms transfer as
architectural arguments; the effect sizes do not transfer at all. This
literature makes the substrate thesis *plausible* and defeats the strong form of
the recency objection. It does not confirm the thesis, and we do not claim it
does.

## 6. What would falsify this

Named before the answer is known:

1. **A major provider ships genuinely portable memory** — documented format,
   re-importable, covering the inferred profile and not only raw transcripts,
   with user-held governance. The §4 argument would be void. Note this now
   requires going *beyond* what GDPR Art. 20 and Data Act Recital 15 oblige
   [10,11], which is why we consider it unlikely rather than impossible.
2. **Re-projection proves intractable at decade scale.** The residue of §5.1.
   Measurable on synthetic corpora; unmeasured.
3. **A controlled evaluation shows recency dominates for memory tasks.** If a
   one-year window matches a ten-year corpus on genuinely personal tasks, the
   compounding premise fails. §5.6 makes this less likely for trait-level
   structure but leaves it wide open for episodic recall.
4. **Temporal framing computed in code shows no advantage** over leaving
   temporal arithmetic to the model. Subject of paper 002, designed as an
   ablation on public data so a negative result is publishable.
5. **Capture friction proves fatal in practice.** If long-run retention among
   users resembles the ~50% abandonment observed in personal informatics [9],
   the architecture is correct and irrelevant. This is the one we expect to be
   tested first.

## 7. Souvenance as an instantiation

Brief by intent: the paper argues an architecture; the implementation is
evidence that it runs, not the subject.

Events land in an append-only PostgreSQL log; memories, embeddings and a
bi-temporal knowledge graph are projections recomputed from it; nightly
consolidation runs on a swappable backend; the memory is served to any client
over MCP. It runs on the author's own hardware against a real personal corpus,
open source under MIT.

Its evidential value is limited and we do not overstate it: n=1, no controlled
comparison, and the author is not a neutral evaluator of their own design. It
demonstrates feasibility. It demonstrates nothing about outcomes.

## 8. Conclusion

The bet is not that today's models are good enough. It is that they are not, and
that this is the argument for building now rather than against it.

If the intelligent layer is going to be replaced — by the labs' own roadmaps and
by the critics who intend to replace the architecture entirely — then coupling a
personal memory to any generation of it is the error. What is worth owning is
the layer that does not improve on its own and cannot be regenerated later: the
record itself, held by the subject, in a format every future engine can read.

The strongest evidence for this is not philosophical. It is that at industrial
scale, when the choice was forced, the system that compressed its history into a
fixed memory hit a wall and the system that kept everything and searched it
conditionally did not [6,7]. The labs building distilled personal memory are
running the architecture that already lost that comparison — for the layer where
it matters most, and where the data is not recoverable when they change their
minds.
